## 114. Engineering Case Studies II: Real-Shaped Incidents in Distributed Systems and Scale

### 114.1 Case: The Sharding Key That Created a Hot Partition

**Context:** A multi-tenant SaaS platform sharded its primary dataset by `tenant_id` (§35's sharding mechanics), a reasonable-looking choice since most queries were naturally scoped to a single tenant. **What happened:** One enterprise customer grew to represent over 40% of total platform activity, and because that customer's `tenant_id` hashed to a single shard, that one shard became a severe hot partition (Part V §91.B) — its database instance was consistently at 95%+ CPU while other shards sat comfortably under 20%, and query latency for that one customer (and, due to shared infrastructure, some neighboring smaller tenants co-located on the same physical shard) degraded significantly. **Root cause:** The sharding key was chosen based on query-scoping convenience (§35.2) without modeling the actual, and highly skewed, distribution of load *across* tenant IDs — a classic case of a sharding key that's logically clean but operationally unbalanced. **Resolution:** The team implemented a hybrid approach — most tenants remained on hash-based `tenant_id` sharding, but the handful of largest enterprise customers were each given a dedicated, isolated shard (effectively sharding by "tenant, with large tenants getting their own partition" rather than a pure, uniform hash function) — directly the same principle as §103.5's celebrity-account fan-out hybrid, applied to database sharding instead of feed generation. **Lessons Learned:** A sharding key should be validated against actual (or realistically projected) load distribution, not just logical query-scoping convenience — and when load is known or likely to be highly skewed (a small number of very large tenants), a hybrid strategy (uniform sharding for the long tail, dedicated isolation for known-large outliers) is often the correct answer rather than searching for a single hash function that somehow balances an inherently unbalanced distribution.

### 114.2 Case: The Consensus Protocol Misconfiguration

**Context:** A team deployed a 5-node etcd cluster (§36's consensus chapter) to back their service-discovery and configuration system, using the default quorum configuration. **What happened:** During a routine cloud-provider maintenance event, 3 of the 5 nodes (which happened to be co-located in a way the team hadn't realized shared an underlying failure domain) went down simultaneously, and — because a Raft-based quorum (§36.3) requires a majority of nodes to be available, and only 2 of 5 remained — the entire cluster lost quorum and became unable to accept writes, even though 2 nodes were technically still healthy and running. **Root cause:** The 5 nodes were provisioned without deliberate attention to failure-domain diversity (availability zones, §59) — three of them happened to share an underlying zone that experienced the maintenance event together, meaning the cluster's nominal "tolerates 2 failures out of 5" guarantee was, in practice, "tolerates failures unless they happen to hit the specific 3 nodes sharing a failure domain," a much weaker real guarantee than the node count suggested. **Resolution:** Redeployed the cluster with nodes deliberately spread across independent availability zones (at most 1-2 nodes per zone, ensuring no single zone failure can take out a quorum-breaking number of nodes), and added explicit monitoring on quorum health and failure-domain distribution as a standing operational check. **Lessons Learned:** A consensus cluster's node *count* only provides its stated fault tolerance if the nodes are also distributed across genuinely independent failure domains — 5 nodes in the same availability zone provide far weaker guarantees than 5 nodes across 3+ zones, even though both configurations report "5 nodes" identically on a dashboard that doesn't surface failure-domain topology.

### 114.3 Case: The Viral Traffic Event That Exceeded Every Capacity Model

**Context:** A content-sharing platform's capacity planning (§56's queueing-theory-based methodology) was based on historical traffic patterns, with headroom sized for roughly 3x normal peak load. **What happened:** A single piece of content went unexpectedly viral, driving traffic to nearly 20x normal peak within about fifteen minutes — far exceeding the planned headroom, and the platform's autoscaling (already a known, separate concern from §112.2's warm-up-time case study) couldn't provision and warm up new capacity fast enough to keep pace with a spike this steep, resulting in a significant availability degradation during the event's peak. **Root cause:** The capacity model was built entirely from historical, organic-growth traffic patterns and had no mechanism for anticipating or absorbing a genuinely viral, near-instantaneous spike — this is a fundamentally different traffic shape than the gradual, plannable growth §56's queueing-theory methodology was designed around. **Resolution:** The team added a specific "viral content" detection and pre-emptive scaling trigger (scaling out aggressively based on the *rate of increase* in engagement on a specific piece of content, not just aggregate system-wide load, allowing capacity to be added ahead of the full traffic spike reaching the system broadly) and additionally implemented graceful degradation for the specific, most expensive personalization/recommendation features (§112.3's bulkhead-isolation principle, applied proactively) so that even under extreme load, core content-viewing functionality could continue serving while lower-priority features shed load first. **Lessons Learned:** Capacity planning based purely on historical, gradual growth patterns cannot anticipate qualitatively different traffic shapes (a viral spike) — systems whose business model makes such spikes plausible need both content/engagement-based predictive scaling triggers (not just load-based reactive ones) and an explicit, pre-planned degradation strategy for which features shed first when true peak capacity is exceeded, rather than treating every feature as equally essential during an extreme event.

### 114.4 Case: The Cross-Region Latency Nobody Measured Until Launch

**Context:** A team building a global application deployed their primary database in a single region (chosen based on where the founding team was located) but deployed application servers across three regions for latency reasons, assuming the application-server-to-database latency would be "probably fine." **What happened:** Users in the two non-primary regions experienced meaningfully worse latency than users in the primary region — not because of anything wrong with the application servers themselves, but because every single database query from those regions incurred a full cross-region network round-trip (§59's global networking chapter), often 100-150ms each way, and a typical page load requiring several sequential queries compounded this into a genuinely poor experience specifically for those users. **Root cause:** The team validated CDN and static-asset latency across regions before launch but never explicitly measured or modeled *database* round-trip latency from non-primary application regions — an implicit assumption that "multi-region app servers" alone would solve global latency, without accounting for where the actual data lived. **Resolution:** Introduced read replicas in the two non-primary regions (§34) for the majority of read-heavy queries, keeping writes routed to the primary region's database — a moderate, incremental fix that didn't require the full complexity of the geo-partitioned, multi-region-write architecture Loop's own capstone only adopted at a much larger scale (§88) once specifically justified. **Lessons Learned:** Deploying application servers in multiple regions solves only part of the global-latency problem — any request path that reaches back to a single-region data store still incurs that store's location's latency penalty, and this must be explicitly measured (not assumed away) before launch, especially for data-heavy pages requiring multiple sequential queries where round-trip latency compounds.

### 114.5 Engineering Intuition

> **What's the common thread across §114.1 and §114.2?** Both are cases where a distributed-systems configuration looked correct on paper (a sharding key, a node count) but failed to account for the *actual, real-world distribution* underlying it (tenant load skew, physical failure-domain placement) — the lesson in both cases is that distributed-systems guarantees are only as strong as the assumptions about real-world distribution that back them, and those assumptions must be explicitly validated, not assumed.

> **What's the common thread across §114.3 and §114.4?** Both are cases of a capacity or latency model built from one traffic/access pattern (gradual historical growth, static-asset delivery) silently failing to generalize to a different pattern (viral spikes, database-bound dynamic queries) that the model was never actually validated against — the lesson is to explicitly state which traffic/access pattern a capacity or latency model was built from, and treat any pattern outside that scope as unvalidated, not safely covered by extension.
