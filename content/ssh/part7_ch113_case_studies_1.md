## 113. Engineering Case Studies I: Real-Shaped Incidents Across the Stack

### 113.1 How These Case Studies Differ from §111-112's Debugging Exercises

§111-112 practiced diagnosing a live incident from an alert. This chapter and §114 zoom out one level — each case study covers a full arc from an engineering *decision*, through its consequences, to the lesson generalized, closer to how a real postmortem or engineering retrospective actually reads (§57.6's ADR/postmortem structure, applied narratively).

### 113.2 Case: The Premature Microservices Migration

**Context:** A 12-person engineering team, building a moderately successful B2B SaaS product with a single monolithic application, decided to split into 15 microservices over six months, motivated by "microservices are what scaling companies do." **What happened:** Deployment complexity increased sharply — a single logical feature now required coordinated changes across 3-4 services; local development required running the entire service mesh, slowing onboarding; and a distributed-transaction bug (an order-processing flow spanning 4 services with no saga/compensation logic, §41.5) caused several instances of inconsistent state that took days to reconcile manually. Team velocity, measured by features shipped per quarter, dropped by roughly a third in the two quarters following the migration. **Root cause:** §108.1's exact trap — the split was motivated by an aspirational, generic belief ("this is what scaling companies do") rather than a genuine, demonstrated organizational or scaling constraint (§12, §86's actual justification criteria — diffuse team-boundary pressure at a much larger organization size than 12 engineers). **Resolution:** The team consolidated back down to roughly 4 services, drawn along genuine bounded-context lines (§12's domain-driven design principles) rather than the original, more arbitrary 15-way split, and re-established the saga pattern properly for the one workflow that genuinely did need to span service boundaries. **Lessons Learned:** Organizational size and genuine team-boundary friction (§86), not aspiration, should drive a microservices decision — a 12-person team splitting into 15 services has, by construction, created more service boundaries than it has teams to own them, guaranteeing coordination overhead with no corresponding organizational benefit.

### 113.3 Case: The Database Migration That Doubled Downtime

**Context:** An engineering team needed to migrate their primary relational database to a new, larger instance type to address genuine, measured capacity constraints, and planned a single maintenance window to perform the migration via a full database dump-and-restore. **What happened:** The dump-and-restore process took nearly four times longer than the team's testing on a smaller, staging-sized dataset had suggested, because production data volume and index-rebuild time didn't scale linearly with the staging test's data size — the maintenance window, planned for two hours, ran to nearly eight. **Root cause:** The migration rehearsal was performed against a dataset far smaller than production, and the team extrapolated migration time linearly from that test without accounting for index-rebuild time's non-linear relationship with data volume, nor for the specific storage-engine mechanics (§31's B-Tree/LSM-Tree internals) that make index construction time scale worse than linearly for very large tables. **Resolution:** For a second, later migration, the team instead used a live-replication-based migration strategy (setting up the new instance as a replica, letting it catch up while the old primary continued serving traffic, then performing a brief, coordinated cutover, §34's replication mechanics) rather than a dump-and-restore — reducing the actual downtime window from hours to under a minute. **Lessons Learned:** Any migration rehearsal must be performed against production-representative data volume, not a conveniently smaller test set, since several of the most expensive migration steps (index rebuilding specifically) don't scale linearly — and a replication-based cutover strategy should be the default consideration for any primary-database migration where minimizing downtime matters, rather than dump-and-restore.

### 113.4 Case: The Caching Layer That Made an Outage Worse

**Context:** A content platform added an aggressive caching layer in front of their database to handle growing read traffic, with a long TTL (24 hours) chosen specifically to minimize database load. **What happened:** A data-correction bug caused incorrect content to be served to users for nearly a full day after the underlying data had already been fixed in the database, because the long-TTL cache continued serving the stale, incorrect version until each individual cache entry's TTL naturally expired — and because there was no cache-invalidation mechanism tied to the actual data-correction event, only time-based expiry. **Root cause:** The caching strategy optimized purely for database-load reduction (a real, legitimate goal) without considering the operational cost of *not* being able to invalidate specific entries on demand when a correction was needed — a design gap invisible until the first time a correction was actually needed. **Resolution:** Added explicit, event-driven cache invalidation (when a piece of content is updated, actively invalidate its specific cache entry rather than relying solely on TTL expiry) alongside the existing TTL-based expiry as a safety net, giving the team both efficient default caching *and* the ability to immediately correct a served error when one occurs. **Lessons Learned:** A caching strategy chosen purely to minimize backend load, without an explicit invalidation mechanism for the "we need to fix this specific piece of data right now" case, will eventually turn an ordinary data-correction into an extended incident — cache design should always include an answer to "how do we force this specific entry to update immediately," not just "how long should this naturally live."

### 113.5 Case: The Overly Permissive Service Account

**Context:** During a rapid feature-development period, an engineer needed a backend service to write to a specific storage bucket, and rather than scoping a narrow IAM policy to that specific bucket and operation, granted the service account broad read/write access across the entire cloud storage account "to save time and revisit it later." **What happened:** Several months later, a dependency in that same service was compromised via a supply-chain vulnerability (§49's software-supply-chain security chapter), and the attacker used the compromised service's overly broad credentials to access and exfiltrate data from several unrelated storage buckets the service had no legitimate reason to touch. **Root cause:** A least-privilege violation (§49's core security principle) introduced under time pressure and never revisited — "temporary" broad permissions are a common and specifically dangerous pattern because the revisit rarely happens once the immediate feature ships and attention moves elsewhere. **Resolution:** Beyond remediating the specific incident, the team implemented automated, periodic IAM-policy auditing specifically flagging overly broad grants for required review, rather than relying on individual engineers to remember to narrow scope later. **Lessons Learned:** "Temporary" broad permissions should be treated as a tracked technical-debt item with an actual follow-up deadline, not an informal intention — and the blast radius of any single compromised credential is a direct, measurable function of how narrowly that credential's permissions were scoped in the first place, making least-privilege a control that specifically limits *consequence*, not just *likelihood*, of a security incident.

### 113.6 Engineering Intuition

> **What's the common thread across §113.2 and §113.3?** Both are cases of extrapolating from an unrepresentative test — aspirational reasoning in §113.2's case (extrapolating "what scaling companies do" without the actual scale), and a too-small test dataset in §113.3's case — the lesson in both is that decisions should be validated against your *actual* situation's evidence, not a proxy for it.

> **What's the common thread across §113.4 and §113.5?** Both are cases where a reasonable-looking optimization (aggressive caching, quick permission grants) omitted the specific mechanism needed to *undo* or *correct* that optimization's effect when something went wrong — a durable engineering habit is asking "how do I reverse this specific decision quickly if I need to" at the time the decision is made, not after the first time it's needed.
