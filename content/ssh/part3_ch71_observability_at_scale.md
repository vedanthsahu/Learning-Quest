## 71. Observability at Scale: Sampling, Cardinality Explosions, Cost of Telemetry at Scale

### 71.1 What This Chapter Adds to §16 and §48

§16 and §48 covered observability's purpose and mechanics. This chapter covers what changes once telemetry volume itself — metrics, logs, and traces generated by thousands of services handling enormous request volume — becomes a genuine cost and engineering problem in its own right, requiring deliberate tradeoffs rather than "collect everything."

### 71.2 The Cardinality Explosion Problem

**Cardinality**, in the observability context, refers to the number of distinct combinations of label/tag values a metric can take on — a metric tagged only by service name might have a cardinality of a few hundred (one per service), while a metric additionally tagged by user ID could have a cardinality in the millions. Metrics systems (§48.2) are typically architected around the assumption of comparatively low cardinality, and adding a high-cardinality dimension (a user ID, a full request path with dynamic segments, an IP address) to a metric can cause the underlying storage and query system's resource usage to explode disproportionately — not linearly, but often combinatorially, as every unique combination of label values requires its own separately-tracked time series. This is why observability platforms at scale enforce explicit cardinality limits and require deliberate justification before adding a new high-cardinality label, treating cardinality itself as a scarce, carefully-rationed resource rather than a free dimension to tag data with whenever convenient.

### 71.3 Sampling: Accepting Incomplete Data Deliberately

At sufficient request volume, capturing and storing complete telemetry for every single request (especially full distributed traces, §48.4, which are comparatively large and expensive per-request) becomes cost-prohibitive — the storage and processing cost of telemetry can, counterintuitively, approach or even exceed the cost of the production traffic it's describing. **Sampling** deliberately captures only a fraction of requests' full telemetry (e.g., 1% of all traces in full detail), while typically still aggregating metrics (§48.2) across the *complete*, unsampled traffic, since metrics' pre-aggregated nature makes them far cheaper to collect at full volume than complete, per-request traces or logs. A critical, easy-to-get-wrong design detail: uniform random sampling risks losing visibility into rare but important events (a rare error occurring in only 0.01% of requests might simply never appear in a 1%-sampled trace set), which is why more sophisticated approaches use **biased sampling** — deliberately over-sampling requests that are already known to be unusual (an error occurred, latency exceeded a threshold) while under-sampling the large volume of routine, healthy requests that carry comparatively little additional diagnostic value once a representative sample of them has been captured.

### 71.4 Log Volume Management: Structured Logging's Cost at Scale

§48.5 established structured logging's diagnostic value. At hyperscale request volume, the sheer storage and processing cost of retaining full structured logs for every request, indefinitely, becomes substantial — leading to deliberate **log level** discipline (only routinely retaining warnings and errors at full detail and full retention, while routine informational logs are either sampled, aggregated, or retained for a much shorter window) and tiered storage strategies (recent logs in fast, expensive, immediately-queryable storage; older logs moved to slower, cheaper archival storage, retained for compliance or rare deep-historical investigation but not optimized for fast, everyday querying) — directly the same hot/cold data tiering principle appearing elsewhere in this book (§31.6's buffer pool, holding only the actively "hot" working set in the fastest storage tier) now applied to observability data itself.

### 71.5 The Meta-Problem: Observability Infrastructure Itself Needs Observability and Capacity Planning

A genuinely large-scale-specific problem: the observability platform itself becomes a substantial piece of production infrastructure, subject to the same capacity planning (§56), reliability engineering (§52), and cost management (§68) disciplines as any other system — and an under-provisioned or degraded observability platform is a particularly dangerous kind of failure, because it can silently blind incident responders (§57) at precisely the moment (a major, cascading incident, §64.4) when observability data is most needed and most likely to be under unusually heavy load itself (since incidents often coincide with spikes in error-logging and trace volume). Mature organizations at this scale explicitly capacity-plan and monitor their observability platform's own health as a first-class, dedicated concern, precisely because "the thing we use to see problems" failing silently during a real problem is a uniquely costly compounding failure.

### 71.6 Common Mistakes and Production Debugging Signals

- Adding a high-cardinality label (a raw user ID, an unbounded dynamic URL path segment) to a metric without considering the combinatorial storage/query cost consequence (§71.2), causing a sudden, severe degradation in the metrics platform's own performance and cost, often disproportionate to the intent behind the change.
- Using uniform random sampling for distributed tracing without biased over-sampling of errors or high-latency requests (§71.3), leaving the system effectively blind to rare but important failure patterns specifically because they're statistically unlikely to appear in an unbiased sample.
- Under-provisioning the observability platform itself relative to production traffic's actual telemetry generation rate (§71.5), causing observability data loss or severe query slowdowns specifically during major incidents, exactly when that data is most critical.

### 71.7 Engineering Intuition

> **How do I know if I have a cardinality problem?** Check whether a metrics platform's storage growth or query latency has increased sharply following the addition of a new label/tag dimension, especially one with a large or unbounded set of possible values (§71.2).
>
> **What symptoms indicate inadequate sampling strategy?** Rare but genuinely important error patterns going unnoticed or undiagnosable because the trace sampling rate happened not to capture representative examples of them — a direct sign that biased, error-aware sampling (§71.3) is needed rather than uniform random sampling.
>
> **What metrics indicate a log volume management gap?** Log storage cost growing disproportionately relative to actual production traffic growth, or query performance against log data degrading as retained volume grows without any corresponding tiering strategy (§71.4).
>
> **What breaks first if the observability platform itself isn't capacity-planned?** During a major incident — precisely when telemetry volume spikes and observability data is most needed — the observability platform itself degrades or drops data, compounding the very incident it should be helping diagnose (§71.5).
>
> **When is simple, complete, unsampled telemetry collection still acceptable?** At request volumes low enough that the storage and processing cost of complete telemetry remains a small, easily-affordable fraction of overall infrastructure cost — sampling and cardinality discipline are costs paid specifically once that volume genuinely makes complete collection impractical.
>
> **What would a hyperscale company do?** Enforce strict cardinality budgets and review processes for new metric dimensions, implement biased, error-aware trace sampling, apply tiered log retention strategies, and capacity-plan and monitor their observability platform with the same rigor as any other critical production system (§79).
>
> **What would a two-person startup do?** Use a managed observability platform's default retention and sampling settings without custom tuning, relying on the platform provider to have made reasonable default choices appropriate for their comparatively modest telemetry volume.
>
> **What changes with scale?** At low request volume, complete, untiered telemetry collection is affordable and simple. At hyperscale volume, the deliberate tradeoffs in this chapter — cardinality budgeting, biased sampling, tiered retention, and dedicated observability-platform capacity planning — become necessary to keep observability itself affordable and reliable precisely when it matters most (§79).

### 71.8 Exercises

1. A team adds a raw user ID as a label on a frequently-emitted metric, and shortly afterward, their metrics platform's cost and query latency both increase sharply. Using §71.2, explain the mechanism behind this and propose an alternative approach that preserves useful per-user diagnostic capability without the same cardinality cost.
2. Explain, using §71.3, why uniform random sampling of distributed traces at a 1% rate could cause a rare but serious bug (occurring in roughly 1 in 10,000 requests) to go completely undetected in trace data, and how biased sampling would address this.
