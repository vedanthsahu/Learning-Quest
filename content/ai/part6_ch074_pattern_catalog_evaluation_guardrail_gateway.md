## 74. Pattern Catalog VII: Evaluation, Guardrail & Gateway Patterns

### 74.1 Evaluation Pipeline Pattern

**Business Problem:** Ad hoc, manual quality checking doesn't scale and doesn't reliably catch regressions before they reach production. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §29's mechanics — a golden dataset (§29.2) run automatically against every change, producing quality/cost/latency/safety scores (§29.7) that gate deployment. **Architecture:** §52.9's deployment-gate pattern — candidate pipeline run against the golden dataset, scored, compared against a baseline threshold, blocking deployment on regression. **Tradeoffs, Failure Modes, Production Signals:** See §29, §39 in full — including §39's evaluation-at-scale concerns (dataset freshness, runtime cost) as this pipeline matures. **When NOT to Use It:** Never appropriate to skip for any product past initial prototyping (§52.1's motivating incident is the direct cautionary case). **Real-World Examples:** §52's Nova Stage 9. **Interview Discussion:** A near-mandatory component of any complete interview answer's Step 7 — its *absence* from a proposed architecture is a stronger interview signal (negative) than its presence is a positive one, since it's considered baseline practice. **Cross-References:** §12, §29, §39, §52.

### 74.2 Guardrail Pipeline Pattern

**Business Problem:** Natural-language input and output require validation that ordinary structured-data validation (companion §49) can't directly provide. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §30.5's layered mechanism — rule-based, classifier-based, and model-based guardrail layers applied to input and output, each targeting different risk/cost tradeoffs. **Architecture:** §53.4's Nova Stage 10 layered design — targeted defenses matched to specific, identified risks rather than one uniform blanket check. **Tradeoffs, Failure Modes, Production Signals:** See §30.5, §40 in full. **When NOT to Use It:** Never entirely skippable for a production system handling any untrusted input, though the specific layer combination should be matched to actual risk (§53.3's Option A vs. B tradeoff) rather than maximized indiscriminately. **Real-World Examples:** §53's Nova Stage 10. **Interview Discussion:** Should be discussed as *layered and risk-targeted* (§30.2), not a single monolithic check — proposing one comprehensive guardrail model for every request, regardless of risk level, misses §53.3's cost-efficiency lesson. **Cross-References:** §13.5, §30.5, §40, §53.

### 74.3 Human Review Loop Pattern

**Business Problem:** Some actions or outputs carry consequences too significant to leave to fully autonomous AI judgment, regardless of how well-guardrailed the system is. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §25.8's human-in-the-loop principle — an explicit checkpoint requiring human approval before a high-stakes or hard-to-reverse action proceeds. **Architecture:** §53.9's policy-engine-gated pattern — a structural check (not a prompt instruction) routing specific actions to a human queue rather than autonomous execution, based on action type, amount, or role (§30.10's refund-cap example). **Tradeoffs:** Meaningfully reduces risk from autonomous errors or manipulation (§13.6) at the direct cost of slower turnaround for the gated actions and the operational cost of maintaining a human-review capacity. **Failure Modes:** Review queues growing faster than human capacity, creating a backlog that either delays users unacceptably or pressures reviewers into rubber-stamping without genuine scrutiny (defeating the pattern's purpose). **Production Signals:** Review-queue depth and average review turnaround time, tracked alongside the rate at which reviewed actions are actually rejected (a near-zero rejection rate may indicate the threshold for triggering review is set too conservatively, gating actions that don't actually need it). **When NOT to Use It:** Low-stakes, easily-reversible actions where the review overhead isn't justified by the risk — over-gating (§53.6's cost) is a real failure mode of this pattern, not just under-gating. **Real-World Examples:** §53.4/§65.1/§65.4's Nova, email, and financial-assistant examples. **Interview Discussion:** Essential for every high-stakes worked example (§65) — its absence in an answer for a healthcare or financial assistant design is a clear, immediate gap. **Cross-References:** §25.8, §30.10, §53.

### 74.4 Inference Gateway / LLM Gateway Pattern

**Business Problem:** An organization using multiple model providers, multiple internal teams, and multiple deployed AI features needs a consistent point of control for authentication, rate limiting, cost tracking, and routing — rather than each feature independently implementing its own provider integration. **Engineering Motivation:** A centralized service sitting between every internal AI feature and the actual model providers (closed APIs and/or self-hosted serving, §27), providing a single, consistent interface regardless of which underlying model or provider ultimately serves a given request — directly the AI-specific instance of the companion handbook's API-gateway pattern (companion §52's API design principles), and the same standardization motivation underlying §25.7's MCP for tools. **Architecture:** Every AI feature calls the gateway rather than a provider directly; the gateway handles authentication, applies rate limiting (§31.8) and cost tracking (§31.4) uniformly, and routes to the appropriate underlying model/provider (potentially applying §68.4's routing pattern or §68.6's fallback pattern centrally rather than per-feature). **Tradeoffs:** Consistent, centralized observability (§41.4's shared-infrastructure health view becomes trivial to build once every feature routes through one gateway) and policy enforcement versus a new, critical-path shared dependency whose own health directly affects every AI feature in the organization. **Failure Modes:** The gateway itself becoming a single point of failure or a latency bottleneck if not engineered for the aggregate load of every feature routing through it; inconsistent adoption (some features bypassing the gateway) undermining the centralized-observability benefit entirely. **Production Signals:** Gateway-specific latency overhead (the cost this centralization adds per request) tracked explicitly, and adoption rate across internal features (§41.9's shared-schema-adoption discipline, directly applicable here). **When NOT to Use It:** A single-feature, single-provider product early in its lifecycle (§44's Nova Stage 1) where the coordination benefit doesn't yet exist to capture — this pattern earns its complexity specifically at the point (§41's Part III chapter) where multiple AI features sharing infrastructure actually exist. **Real-World Examples:** §41.4's shared-infrastructure health view directly presupposes something like this pattern already existing; commercial "LLM gateway" products exist specifically to provide this function off-the-shelf. **Interview Discussion:** The correct answer to "how would you manage multiple AI features across an organization consistently" — a candidate proposing per-feature, independent provider integration for an organization with many AI features has missed this consolidation opportunity. **Cross-References:** §27.5, §31.4, §31.8, §41.4, §25.7.

### 74.5 Cost-Aware and Latency-Aware Routing Patterns

**Business Problem:** §68.4's general model-routing pattern can be specialized around one specific optimization axis when that axis dominates the routing decision for a given product. **Engineering Motivation — Cost-Aware Routing:** Routes primarily to minimize spend for a given quality bar, appropriate when a product's unit economics are tight and quality requirements are relatively uniform across request types (§33's cost-engineering discipline driving the routing logic directly). **Engineering Motivation — Latency-Aware Routing:** Routes primarily to meet a hard latency ceiling, appropriate when user experience is acutely latency-sensitive for specific request types (directly §59.4's inline-completion example, where latency, not cost, is the dominant routing criterion). **Architecture:** Both are specializations of §68.4's routing pattern with a different primary objective function driving the routing decision — cost-aware routing optimizes against §15.13's cost-estimation chain; latency-aware routing optimizes against §15.7's latency chain. **Tradeoffs:** A router single-mindedly optimizing cost may violate latency expectations for time-sensitive requests, and vice versa — a mature system often needs *both* considerations combined into one routing policy (a genuine multi-objective optimization) rather than choosing one axis exclusively. **Failure Modes:** Optimizing the wrong axis for a given request type (routing a latency-critical inline-completion request through a cost-minimizing path that happens to be slow, or routing a cost-sensitive batch task through a latency-optimized but expensive path). **Production Signals:** Segmented cost and latency metrics (§31.4, §31.6) per route, explicitly checked against which objective that route was designed to optimize. **When NOT to Use It:** Low-volume products where the routing infrastructure's engineering cost exceeds any realistic optimization gain (§68.4's general caveat applies identically here). **Real-World Examples:** §59.4-59.6's coding-assistant dual-mode routing is simultaneously a latency-aware routing example (inline completion) and a capability-aware one (agentic chat). **Interview Discussion:** Naming the *specific* optimization objective (cost vs. latency vs. capability) driving a proposed routing scheme, rather than saying "we'll route intelligently" vaguely, is what separates a strong answer from a superficial one. **Cross-References:** §68.4, §15.7, §15.13, §33, §59.4.

### 74.6 Safety Pipeline Pattern

**Business Problem:** Safety-specific concerns (content policy violations, harmful output, PII exposure) benefit from a dedicated, auditable pipeline stage distinct from general quality guardrails (§74.2), specifically because safety failures often carry different stakes and different regulatory/compliance requirements than quality failures. **Engineering Motivation:** A dedicated safety-classification stage (distinct from, though sometimes overlapping with, §74.2's guardrail pipeline) specifically producing an auditable safety verdict and rationale for every request/response, often required for compliance/audit purposes beyond what a general guardrail's pass/fail signal provides. **Architecture:** Output passes through a dedicated safety classifier (§30.5's model-based guardrail layer, specifically scoped to safety/policy dimensions) producing a structured, logged verdict, separate from general quality/faithfulness guardrails, specifically so safety decisions can be audited independently later. **Tradeoffs:** Clear audit trail and regulatory defensibility versus the added latency/cost of a dedicated pipeline stage beyond general guardrails. **Failure Modes:** Conflating safety and general-quality guardrails into one undifferentiated check, losing the specific auditability that regulatory or high-stakes contexts (§65.3-65.4) require. **Production Signals:** Safety-verdict distribution and audit-log completeness, tracked as its own compliance-relevant metric distinct from general quality metrics (§29.7). **When NOT to Use It:** Low-stakes, non-regulated products where §74.2's general guardrail pipeline already provides sufficient, undifferentiated safety coverage without needing a separately auditable stage. **Real-World Examples:** §65.3's healthcare assistant, where regulatory audit requirements argue for safety decisions to be explicitly logged and defensible independent of general quality evaluation. **Interview Discussion:** Worth distinguishing explicitly from the general Guardrail Pipeline (§74.2) specifically in regulated-domain worked examples (§65) — conflating the two in a healthcare or financial design answer is a specific, identifiable gap. **Cross-References:** §30.5, §42.5, §65.3-65.4, §74.2.

### 74.7 Engineering Intuition

> **When does an organization need a dedicated Inference/LLM Gateway (§74.4) rather than per-feature provider integration?** Once more than a small handful of AI features exist and need consistent cost tracking, rate limiting, and observability — exactly the threshold §41's Part III chapter identified for needing shared-infrastructure health monitoring in the first place.

> **What would over-engineering look like across this chapter?** Building a dedicated Safety Pipeline (§74.6) separate from general guardrails (§74.2) for a low-stakes, unregulated product where the distinction provides no real audit or compliance benefit — reserve this separation for domains where it's actually load-bearing (§65's healthcare/financial examples).
