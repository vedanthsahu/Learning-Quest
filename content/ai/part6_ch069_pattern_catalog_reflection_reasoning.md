## 69. Pattern Catalog II: Reflection & Reasoning Patterns

### 69.1 Reflection Pattern

**Business Problem:** A model's first-pass output on a complex task is often improvable, but nothing in a single-prompt architecture (§68.2) gives it a chance to catch and fix its own mistakes. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §7.4/§24.4/§25.4 — having the model (or a second call) critique and revise a prior output before finalizing it. **Architecture:** Generate → self-critique against explicit criteria → revise, either as one extended reasoning process or as separate model calls. **Tradeoffs:** Improved quality for tasks where self-critique is reliable, at the direct cost of additional latency and tokens for every request, not just the ones that needed correction (§25.4's stated tradeoff). **Failure Modes:** A model's self-critique being no more reliable than its original output (reflection doesn't help if the model can't actually recognize its own error class); reflection adding cost without measurable quality gain if applied indiscriminately. **Production Signals:** Quality improvement (§29) measured specifically with-vs-without reflection, not assumed; latency/cost increase tracked per request. **When NOT to Use It:** Simple, low-stakes tasks where the added latency/cost isn't justified by the marginal quality gain — validate this empirically (§29) rather than assuming reflection always helps. **Real-World Examples:** §51.9's Nova agentic goal-completion reflection; §23.8's Self-RAG's trained-in version of the same idea. **Interview Discussion:** A strong answer to "how do you improve output quality without changing the model" — but should be paired with an explicit acknowledgment of its cost, not presented as a free win. **Cross-References:** §7.4, §24.4, §25.4, §23.8.

### 69.2 Generator-Critic Pattern

**Business Problem:** A single model both generating and judging its own output risks a blind spot — the same weaknesses that produced a flawed output may also prevent the model from recognizing that flaw. **Engineering Motivation:** Separating generation and criticism into two distinct roles (sometimes two different models, sometimes the same model with a very differently-framed prompt) — directly related to but more architecturally explicit than plain Reflection (§69.1), and closely related to LLM-as-Judge's methodology (§12.3, §29.3) applied inline during generation rather than only in offline evaluation. **Architecture:** Generator produces a candidate output; a separate Critic component (a different model, or the same model with critic-specific instructions and, ideally, different context) scores or critiques it; the Generator revises based on that critique. **Tradeoffs:** A genuinely independent critic (a different model) reduces the shared-blind-spot risk of Reflection at the cost of an additional model's latency/cost, versus a same-model critic which is cheaper but risks the same blind spots reflection alone has. **Failure Modes:** Using the same underlying model for both roles without meaningfully different framing, producing an illusion of independent verification that doesn't actually exist. **Production Signals:** Track critic-generator agreement rate — a critic that virtually always approves the generator's first output is providing little real signal and may not justify its added cost. **When NOT to Use It:** When a genuinely independent, differently-trained critic isn't available or affordable, and the same-model reflection pattern (§69.1) provides most of the achievable benefit for less cost. **Real-World Examples:** RAGAS-style faithfulness scoring (§29.6) used as an inline generation gate rather than only offline; a coding assistant's generated code checked by a separate test-execution "critic" step (§59.5). **Interview Discussion:** A more sophisticated answer than plain Reflection when asked to defend output quality for a high-stakes generation task — worth explicitly contrasting the independence argument against Reflection's cost-efficiency argument. **Cross-References:** §12.3, §29.3, §69.1.

### 69.3 Critic Loop Pattern

**Business Problem:** A single generate-critique-revise cycle (§69.2) may be insufficient for tasks requiring multiple rounds of refinement before quality is acceptable. **Engineering Motivation:** Extending Generator-Critic into a bounded, iterative loop — generate, critique, revise, critique again — continuing until the critic approves or a step limit is reached. **Architecture:** Directly structurally identical to §25.11's agent loop with a `max_steps` safeguard, but with "critic approval" as the specific goal-completion condition rather than a general task-completion check. **Tradeoffs:** Higher achievable quality ceiling versus unbounded-in-advance latency/cost per request — exactly §36's agent-reliability cost/latency-variance concern, now applied to a quality-refinement loop specifically rather than a tool-using agent. **Failure Modes:** The exact looping-forever risk §36.2-36.6 catalogs, now for a critique loop instead of a tool-calling loop — a critic that never approves (miscalibrated too strictly) or a generator that can't address the critic's specific feedback produces the same non-termination risk. **Production Signals:** Iterations-per-request distribution (directly §36.4's steps-per-request metric, renamed for this context) — a rising average or a growing tail hitting the step limit is the same early-warning signal §36.4 identifies. **When NOT to Use It:** Tasks where a single Reflection or Generator-Critic pass already achieves acceptable quality — apply the same "evidence before complexity" discipline (§67) that governs every escalation in this catalog. **Real-World Examples:** Iterative code-generation-and-test-fixing loops (§59.5); iterative document-drafting-and-review workflows. **Interview Discussion:** Directly reuse §36's entire diagnostic framework when discussing this pattern's failure modes in an interview — it is, structurally, the same reliability problem in new clothing. **Cross-References:** §25.11, §36, §69.2.

### 69.4 Planning Pattern

**Business Problem:** A complex, multi-step task benefits from an explicit, reviewable plan before execution begins, rather than deciding each step reactively as work proceeds. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §25.3's planning discussion — explicit upfront planning trades adaptiveness for auditability and control, valuable specifically for high-stakes or genuinely multi-step tasks. **Architecture:** The model first generates a complete, structured plan (a sequence of sub-goals or steps); this plan can be logged, reviewed (potentially by a human, §25.8), or validated before any actual execution/tool-calling begins. **Tradeoffs:** §25.3's stated tradeoff directly — more controllable and inspectable, but less adaptive if an early step's real-world result invalidates the original plan's later steps. **Failure Modes:** A plan generated without sufficient information, becoming invalid partway through execution with no mechanism to re-plan (unlike ReAct's, §69.5, natural adaptiveness). **Production Signals:** Rate of plan invalidation/re-planning needed mid-execution — a high rate suggests the task's actual uncertainty was underestimated and implicit, adaptive planning (ReAct, §69.5) may fit better. **When NOT to Use It:** Short, low-stakes tasks where ReAct's adaptiveness is more valuable than upfront auditability (§50.4's Nova Stage 7 reasoning for choosing ReAct over explicit planning). **Real-World Examples:** §50.3's explicit-planning option Nova considered but deferred; complex multi-file coding-agent tasks (§59.5) that benefit from a reviewable plan before autonomous execution. **Interview Discussion:** The correct answer specifically for high-stakes, hard-to-reverse multi-step tasks (§65.1's email, §65.4's financial examples) where auditability before action matters more than speed. **Cross-References:** §25.3, §25.8, §50.3-50.4.

### 69.5 ReAct Pattern

**Business Problem:** A task requires interleaving reasoning with real actions (tool calls) and adapting based on what those actions reveal — neither pure reasoning nor pure action-taking alone is sufficient. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §24.4/§25's core agent-loop mechanism — reasoning about what to do, acting (a tool call), observing the result, and reasoning again based on that new information. **Architecture:** A loop: reason → act (tool call) → observe result → reason again → ... until a final answer is reached or a step limit hits (§25.11). **Tradeoffs:** Naturally adaptive to unexpected intermediate results (§50.4's stated advantage over upfront planning) at the cost of less auditability/predictability than an explicit plan. **Failure Modes:** The full catalog in §36 — looping, tool-result ambiguity, capability mismatches. **Production Signals:** Exactly §36.4's metrics (steps-per-request, tool-repetition rate). **When NOT to Use It:** Tasks with a known, fixed step sequence (use a Prompt Pipeline, §68.3, instead) or tasks requiring upfront auditability before any action (use explicit Planning, §69.4, instead). **Real-World Examples:** §50.9/§51.9's Nova multi-tool and agentic loop implementations; most documented agentic coding-assistant modes (§59.5). **Interview Discussion:** The default, most commonly expected answer for "how would you build an agent" — a candidate should be able to name it, describe its loop structure, and immediately follow up with its failure modes (§36) unprompted. **Cross-References:** §24.4, §25, §36, §50, §51.

### 69.6 Tree-of-Thought (ToT) and Graph-of-Thought (GoT) Patterns

**Business Problem:** A single linear reasoning chain (Chain-of-Thought, §24.4) can commit early to a suboptimal reasoning path with no mechanism to reconsider. **Engineering Motivation:** ToT (§24.4) explores multiple candidate reasoning branches in parallel, evaluating and pruning before committing to a final path — directly useful for problems with several plausible approaches where the best one isn't obvious upfront. **Graph-of-Thought** extends this further, allowing reasoning steps to merge, revisit, and combine insights across branches (a graph rather than a strict tree structure), useful for problems where insights from different reasoning paths need to be *combined*, not just compared and the best one selected. **Architecture:** Multiple parallel reasoning generations, an evaluation/scoring step comparing them, and either a selection (ToT) or a merging/synthesis step (GoT) producing the final answer. **Tradeoffs:** Meaningfully higher token/cost consumption than linear CoT (multiple reasoning paths generated, not one) in exchange for a materially better result on problems where a single linear path is genuinely insufficient. **Failure Modes:** Applying ToT/GoT to problems where a single CoT pass is already sufficient, paying multiplied cost for no measurable quality gain (§24.8's over-engineering warning, directly applicable). **Production Signals:** Cost-per-request for ToT/GoT-using features tracked and compared explicitly against the quality improvement it purchases (§29.7's cost/quality tradeoff, this catalog's clearest instance of it). **When NOT to Use It:** The large majority of tasks, where linear CoT (§24.4) is sufficient — reserved specifically for problems with genuinely multiple plausible solution approaches needing comparison. **Real-World Examples:** Complex planning or puzzle-like reasoning tasks; less common in typical production chat/RAG/agent products than CoT or ReAct. **Interview Discussion:** Naming ToT/GoT correctly for a genuinely complex, multi-approach reasoning problem is a positive signal; proposing it for a straightforward task is a clear over-engineering tell. **Cross-References:** §24.4, §19.7 (reasoning models' trained-in analogue).

### 69.7 Engineering Intuition

> **How do Reflection, Generator-Critic, and Critic Loop differ in practice?** Reflection is the same model critiquing itself once; Generator-Critic separates the two roles for greater independence; Critic Loop repeats Generator-Critic iteratively until approval or a step limit — increasing sophistication (and cost) in that order, each justified only by evidence the simpler predecessor is insufficient.

> **What would over-engineering look like across this entire chapter?** Reaching for ToT/GoT (§69.6) or a multi-round Critic Loop (§69.3) before confirming, via evaluation, that a single CoT pass or a single Reflection round is actually inadequate — every pattern in this chapter costs more than its simpler predecessor, and that cost should be evidence-justified, not assumed.
