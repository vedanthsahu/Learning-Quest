## 65. Worked Interview Examples IV: AI Email Assistant, AI Resume Reviewer, AI Healthcare Assistant, AI Financial Assistant

### 65.1 Design an AI Email Assistant (Drafting, Summarization, Triage)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** The assistant operates on a continuously-arriving, personal, and often sensitive data stream (a user's own inbox) rather than a static corpus or a fresh per-session query — the architecture must account for ongoing ingestion (new emails constantly) rather than a one-time indexing step, and privacy/PII handling (§13.4, §30.4) is a default requirement given the inherently personal nature of email content, not an edge case. **Architecture (Step 6):** Retrieval over the user's own email history for context ("reply consistent with how I usually respond to this person," directly §48's long-term/episodic memory pattern applied per-user) plus generation for drafting/summarization plus a triage/classification component (routing/prioritizing incoming email, a simpler classification task not requiring the full generative pipeline). **Security (Step 9):** Per-user data isolation (§54.4's tenant-scoping pattern, applied at the individual-user level rather than organization level) is non-negotiable — one user's email content must never influence another's context or drafts. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Draft autonomy (auto-sending vs. always requiring review) is a direct human-in-the-loop decision (§25.8) with real consequences — an email sent incorrectly is a harder-to-reverse action than most chat responses, arguing strongly for review-before-send as the default, not an autonomous-send default.

### 65.2 Design an AI Resume Reviewer (Feedback, Scoring, Matching)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** If this system's output influences real hiring decisions (scoring, ranking, or matching candidates), it inherits fairness and bias concerns beyond this handbook's core AI security chapters (§13, §30) — a candidate should explicitly flag that evaluation (§29) here must include bias/fairness testing across demographic groups, not just accuracy, a genuinely distinct evaluation dimension from anything emphasized in the handbook's other worked examples. **Architecture (Step 6):** Likely a combination of structured extraction (parsing resume content into comparable fields, a narrower generation task than open-ended chat) plus either RAG-style matching against job descriptions or a more traditional scoring/classification approach — a strong answer explicitly questions whether generative AI is even the right tool for the *scoring* component versus a more traditional, auditable classification model, since scoring decisions affecting real people benefit from more interpretable, auditable methods than open-ended LLM judgment (directly relevant to §29.3's warning that an LLM-as-judge has its own error rate and biases). **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Automation and consistency (removing human reviewer variability) versus auditability and fairness risk — an opaque, high-stakes scoring system is a materially different risk profile than a conversational assistant, and this should visibly change the candidate's guardrail (Step 8) and evaluation (Step 7) emphasis.

### 65.3 Design an AI Healthcare Assistant (Patient-Facing or Clinician-Facing)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** The single most important clarifying question: is this patient-facing (materially higher risk, since incorrect medical information directly affects a person's health decisions) or clinician-facing (an assistant helping a trained professional, who retains final judgment)? This distinction should change nearly every subsequent answer, especially Steps 7-9. **Architecture (Step 6):** Almost certainly strict RAG grounding (§23.9, similar to §63.1's NotebookLM-style strictness) against authoritative medical sources or the specific patient's own records (with §54.4-level access control, since patient data is maximally sensitive), explicitly avoiding reliance on the model's general pretrained medical knowledge, which is not a verified, current, authoritative source. **Evaluation (Step 7):** Faithfulness/groundedness (§29.6) at the highest bar of any example in this chapter, plus mandatory human-expert review during evaluation (§29.4's human-evaluation discipline) rather than relying primarily on LLM-as-judge (§29.3) given the stakes. **Guardrails/Security (Steps 8-9):** Regulatory compliance (HIPAA or equivalent, beyond this handbook's direct scope but directly analogous to §54.1's data-residency-style hard legal constraint) and an explicit, prominent disclaimer/escalation-to-human-clinician mechanism (§63.4's escalation pattern, at much higher stakes) are required, not optional additions. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Directly and explicitly: §42.5's domain-consequence severity weighting applies at maximum intensity here — a hallucination (§35) in this domain has categorically higher real-world consequence than in any other worked example in this chapter, and a strong answer says so explicitly rather than treating this as just another RAG product.

### 65.4 Design an AI Financial Assistant (Advice, Analysis, or Transaction-Related)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** Similarly to healthcare, clarify scope immediately: is this providing general financial information/analysis (lower stakes), specific personalized advice (regulatory implications in many jurisdictions), or actually initiating transactions (§49's tool-calling territory, with real, hard-to-reverse financial consequences)? **Architecture (Step 6):** RAG over the user's own financial data/documents (with strict access control, §54.4) plus, if transaction-capable, tools with the tightest structural constraints in this entire chapter's examples (§30.10's schema-level amount caps are directly the right pattern here, not optional). **Guardrails (Step 8):** Any transaction-initiating capability requires human-in-the-loop confirmation for anything beyond small, pre-authorized amounts (§25.8, §53.4's policy-engine pattern) — directly mirroring §30.10's refund-cap example, now for potentially much larger stakes. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** As in healthcare, the domain-consequence severity (§42.5) argues for conservative defaults (require confirmation, limit autonomous action) even at the cost of a less "seamless" user experience — a financial assistant that feels slightly more effortful to use because it asks for confirmation is a correct, deliberate tradeoff, not a product shortcoming.

### 65.5 The Pattern Across All Four Examples: Stakes Change the Framework's Weighting, Not Its Steps

These four examples share a common lesson distinct from §62-64's: as domain consequence rises (email → resume/hiring → healthcare → finance), the *same twelve-step framework* applies, but Steps 7-9 (Evaluation, Guardrails, Security) deserve proportionally more of the answer's time and depth, and Step 12's tradeoffs increasingly favor conservative, human-confirming defaults over autonomous seamlessness. A candidate who gives healthcare and a simple FAQ bot the same depth of guardrail discussion has failed to calibrate to stakes — exactly the judgment §42.5's severity-classification chapter taught for production incidents, now applied at design time instead of incident-response time.

### 65.6 Engineering Intuition

> **What single question determines how much of an interview answer should focus on guardrails and evaluation rather than architecture?** "What happens if this system is wrong, and who does it happen to?" — the answer to this question should visibly and proportionally reallocate time across the twelve steps, not just be mentioned once and then ignored.

> **What would over-engineering look like for the Resume Reviewer example specifically?** Assuming a generative LLM must be the scoring mechanism, when a more traditional, auditable classification approach might better serve the fairness/auditability requirement that this specific product's stakes demand — a rare case in this handbook where "use a simpler, more traditional method" is the more sophisticated answer.
