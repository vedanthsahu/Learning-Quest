## 73. Pattern Catalog VI: Retrieval Pipeline Patterns

### 73.1 Two-Stage Retrieval Pattern

**Business Problem:** Fast, cheap retrieval (bi-encoder embedding similarity, §21.3) is scalable across a large corpus but less accurate than a slower, more thorough comparison method; running the accurate method against the entire corpus is too slow to be practical. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §21.7's standard production pattern — use cheap retrieval to narrow a large corpus to a small candidate set, then apply a more expensive, more accurate reranker only to that small set. **Architecture:** §21.11's retrieve-then-rerank pipeline — Stage 1 (bi-encoder/hybrid retrieval over the full corpus) → Stage 2 (cross-encoder reranking over the top-k candidates only). **Tradeoffs:** Combines Stage 1's scalability with Stage 2's accuracy at the cost of Stage 2's added latency (proportional to candidate-set size, not full corpus size) and infrastructure complexity. **Failure Modes:** §38.5's diagnosis — if Stage 1's recall is already poor, Stage 2 cannot recover a document that was never retrieved into the candidate set at all. **Production Signals:** §21.8's recall/precision measured separately for Stage 1 alone versus the combined pipeline (§38.7's diagnostic technique). **When NOT to Use It:** Small corpora where Stage 1 alone (or even exhaustive exact search) is already fast and accurate enough that Stage 2's added complexity isn't justified. **Real-World Examples:** §21.11's general pattern, used throughout Nova's RAG stage (§47) once evaluation identified a ranking-precision gap. **Interview Discussion:** The default, expected answer for "how do you make retrieval both fast and accurate" — a candidate should immediately name this two-stage structure rather than trying to make one single method do both jobs. **Cross-References:** §21.3, §21.7, §21.11, §38.5.

### 73.2 Three-Stage Retrieval Pattern

**Business Problem:** At very large corpus scale or with especially demanding precision requirements, even two-stage retrieval's reranking step may need its own coarser pre-filtering to keep the reranker's candidate set manageable, or a distinct filtering stage (metadata/access-control) may need to run independently of relevance ranking entirely. **Engineering Motivation:** Extending §73.1 with an explicit third stage — commonly, a metadata/access-control filter (§13.4, §22.4) applied either before or interleaved with the retrieval stages, ensuring relevance ranking never considers documents a given user/tenant shouldn't see in the first place, distinct from the two-stage pattern's implicit assumption that filtering is "somewhere" in the pipeline. **Architecture:** Stage 1 (metadata/access-control filtering, narrowing the corpus to only permissible documents) → Stage 2 (fast retrieval/embedding similarity over the filtered set) → Stage 3 (reranking over the small candidate set). **Tradeoffs:** Explicit, structural guarantee that access control is enforced before relevance ranking ever runs (directly §30.4's warning that filtering must be structural, not an afterthought) versus the added pipeline complexity of a distinct filtering stage. **Failure Modes:** Filtering applied *after* rather than before/alongside retrieval, meaning ranking computation is wasted on documents that will be discarded anyway, or — far worse — a filtering bug allowing an unauthorized document to be ranked and potentially surfaced (§13.4's data-leakage risk realized). **Production Signals:** §54.9's structural tenant-scoping verification, applied to confirm filtering genuinely runs first. **When NOT to Use It:** Single-tenant systems with no access-control filtering requirement, where §73.1's two-stage pattern already captures the full pipeline. **Real-World Examples:** §54's Nova Stage 11 enterprise architecture, where every retrieval call must pass through tenant-scoped filtering before ranking. **Interview Discussion:** The correct, more complete answer specifically for multi-tenant or access-controlled retrieval systems (§63.3's Enterprise Assistant example) — naming filtering as an explicit pipeline stage, not an assumed detail, is a strong signal. **Cross-References:** §13.4, §22.4, §30.4, §54.

### 73.3 Hybrid Retrieval Pattern

**Business Problem:** Dense (embedding-based) retrieval and sparse/lexical (keyword-based) retrieval have complementary, non-overlapping blind spots — neither alone handles the full range of realistic query types well. **Engineering Motivation:** Directly §20.4/§21.4's mechanism — run both retrieval methods independently and merge their ranked results (typically via Reciprocal Rank Fusion, §21.4), covering dense retrieval's synonym/paraphrase strength and sparse retrieval's exact-term/code/identifier strength simultaneously. **Architecture, Tradeoffs, Failure Modes, Production Signals:** See §21.4, §20.9's decision tree in full. **When NOT to Use It:** Query patterns that are reliably and exclusively one type (purely conceptual queries with no exact-term needs, or purely keyword/code lookups) — confirmed via evaluation (§21.8) before adding a second retrieval method's complexity. **Real-World Examples:** §22.6's Weaviate native support; any RAG system serving both natural-language and technical/code-adjacent queries. **Interview Discussion:** A near-default answer whenever an interview question's corpus mixes prose and structured/technical content (e.g., §64.2's coding assistant, §63.3's enterprise assistant with product codes). **Cross-References:** §20.3-20.4, §21.2, §21.4.

### 73.4 Cross-Encoder Pipeline Pattern

**Business Problem:** For domains where retrieval precision is unusually critical (high-stakes answers, §65.3-65.4) or where the corpus's documents are subtly similar to each other (making bi-encoder similarity alone insufficiently discriminating), even standard two-stage reranking (§73.1) may need a more thorough cross-encoder treatment across a larger portion of the pipeline than usual. **Engineering Motivation:** A deliberate emphasis-shift within the two-stage pattern — allocating a larger reranking candidate set (§38.9's top_k tuning) and/or a stronger, domain-fine-tuned cross-encoder model, explicitly trading additional latency/cost for maximum achievable precision, appropriate specifically when §65's stakes-calibration argues for it. **Architecture:** Structurally identical to §73.1's two-stage pattern, but with Stage 2's candidate-set size and model quality deliberately tuned toward precision over speed, rather than the default balanced tradeoff. **Tradeoffs:** §38.9's recall/latency tradeoff curve, deliberately shifted toward the precision/latency-cost end for domains where correctness stakes justify it. **Failure Modes:** Applying this precision-maximizing configuration uniformly across a product with mixed-stakes queries, paying its latency cost even for low-stakes queries that didn't need it — better handled via Adaptive-style routing (§23.5's pattern, applied to retrieval configuration rather than RAG variant selection) than a uniform default. **Production Signals:** Latency and cost specifically for the reranking stage, tracked and explicitly justified against the domain's stakes (§42.5's severity-weighting principle, applied at design time). **When NOT to Use It:** General-purpose or low-stakes retrieval, where standard two-stage reranking (§73.1) already balances precision and latency adequately. **Real-World Examples:** §65.3's healthcare assistant's retrieval configuration, where grounding precision justifies the added latency cost far more than a general FAQ bot would. **Interview Discussion:** The correct answer when an interview question's domain stakes (§65's healthcare/financial examples) argue explicitly for prioritizing retrieval precision over speed — should be paired with an explicit acknowledgment of the latency cost being deliberately accepted, not overlooked. **Cross-References:** §21.7, §38.9, §23.5, §65.3-65.4.

### 73.5 Engineering Intuition

> **How do Two-Stage, Three-Stage, and Cross-Encoder Pipeline patterns relate?** Three-Stage adds an explicit access-control stage to Two-Stage's core structure; the Cross-Encoder Pipeline pattern is Two-Stage with its reranking stage's precision/latency dial deliberately turned toward precision — all three share the same foundational retrieve-then-rerank shape (§73.1), differing in what's added or emphasized, not in a fundamentally different structure.

> **What would over-engineering look like across this chapter?** Applying the Cross-Encoder Pipeline pattern's precision-maximizing configuration (§73.4) uniformly to every query in a general-purpose, low-stakes product — the added latency cost should be reserved for the specific queries/domains where correctness stakes actually justify it (§65's calibration lesson, applied here).
