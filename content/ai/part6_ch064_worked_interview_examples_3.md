## 64. Worked Interview Examples III: AI Search, AI Coding Assistant, AI Document Chat, AI Meeting Assistant

### 64.1 Design AI Search (Generic, Not a Named Product)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** When asked generically rather than "design Perplexity," the interviewer is likely testing whether the candidate can determine the corpus scope independently — is this searching the open web (§63.2's territory), a specific enterprise's documents (§63.3's territory), or a specific vertical dataset (legal, medical)? A strong answer opens by asking this question rather than assuming. **Architecture (Step 6):** Regardless of corpus, the core mechanism is retrieval (§21) plus grounded generation with citation (§23.9) — the candidate's job is choosing the right retrieval mechanics (§21.2-21.7) for the stated corpus: BM25/hybrid for exact-term-heavy enterprise search, dense/hybrid with reranking for broader semantic search, ANN algorithm choice (§21.5) driven by corpus scale. **Evaluation (Step 7):** Recall@k/Precision@k (§21.8) as the primary metric, independent of generation quality — a candidate who only discusses generation quality for a "search" product has under-weighted the retrieval half of the system. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Retrieval latency versus thoroughness (§21.5's ANN accuracy/speed tradeoff, §21.7's reranking cost) — this generic version of the question is really testing whether a candidate can reason about this tradeoff from first principles rather than reciting Perplexity's specific documented choices.

### 64.2 Design an AI Coding Assistant (Generic, Not Cursor/Copilot)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** As in §64.1, the interviewer wants the candidate to independently surface §62.4's key insight — two distinct latency/architecture regimes (inline completion vs. agentic editing) — rather than being told this distinction exists. A candidate who immediately proposes one uniform agentic architecture for both has missed the derivation the named-product examples (§62.4-62.5) demonstrated. **Architecture (Step 6):** Codebase-aware retrieval (§59.3) — reasoning explicitly about why code's structure (imports, call graphs, symbol definitions) makes naive text chunking (§21.6) potentially insufficient is a strong, differentiating point. **Guardrails (Step 8):** Destructive-action risk from file edits and terminal command execution (§13.6, §30.6) — an answer proposing an agentic coding assistant with no mention of least-privilege scoping or a review checkpoint (§25.8) is incomplete regardless of how sophisticated its retrieval design is. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** As in §62.4 — autonomy versus review friction, the central tension of this entire product category.

### 64.3 Design AI Document Chat (Chat Over an Uploaded Document Set)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** Functionally very close to NotebookLM (§63.1) but often asked in a more general, single-document or small-document-set context (e.g., "chat with this PDF") rather than a broader research-notebook product — the key question to surface is document *scale and structure*: a single short document needs no chunking/retrieval infrastructure at all (it may fit entirely in context, §15.5), while a large multi-document set needs the full RAG pipeline (§21, §47). A candidate who proposes a full vector-database RAG pipeline for a five-page PDF has over-engineered relative to the actual requirement — directly §6.9/§47.8's "is RAG even needed yet" judgment. **Architecture (Step 6):** For small documents: direct context inclusion, no retrieval infrastructure needed. For larger/multiple documents: standard RAG (§21, §23.2-23.3) with strict source grounding (§23.9, similar to §63.1). **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Simplicity (fit-in-context for small documents) versus generality (RAG infrastructure that scales to any document size) — a well-reasoned answer explicitly states the document-size threshold where the tradeoff flips, rather than picking one architecture unconditionally.

### 64.4 Design an AI Meeting Assistant (Transcription, Summarization, Action Items)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** This product has a defining characteristic none of the previous examples share: its primary input is audio, requiring a transcription step (typically a separate speech-to-text model/service) *before* any of this handbook's text-based mechanisms apply at all — a candidate who jumps straight to "we'll use RAG" without first addressing the audio-to-text pipeline has skipped a foundational, product-defining component. **Architecture (Step 6):** Transcription → summarization (a generation task, directly this handbook's core mechanisms once text exists) → action-item extraction (structured output generation, similar in spirit to §48.9's memory-extraction pattern) → optionally, RAG over past meeting transcripts for "what did we discuss about X last month"-style queries (directly §48's long-term memory pattern, applied to meetings instead of conversations). **Latency budget (Step 4):** Real-time or near-real-time transcription during a live meeting has a materially different latency requirement than post-meeting summarization, which can tolerate more processing time — mirroring §62.4's dual-mode distinction in a new domain. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Transcription accuracy (larger, more accurate speech models) versus real-time responsiveness during a live meeting — directly the capability/cost/latency triangle (§1.5) applied to a non-text-generation component this handbook otherwise didn't cover in depth.

### 64.5 The Pattern Across All Four Examples

These four "generic" (non-named-product) questions are deliberately designed to test whether a candidate can perform the derivation §62-63's named-product examples demonstrated, without the product name doing any of the work for them. The strongest tell of genuine understanding versus memorization is exactly this: can the candidate surface the same hidden-constraint questions (corpus scope, latency regime, action risk) on a product they've never specifically studied?

### 64.6 Engineering Intuition

> **What's the single biggest risk in answering a generic version of a question you've studied a named example of?** Silently assuming the named example's specific answer applies without re-deriving it — e.g., assuming "AI Coding Assistant" must have Cursor's exact dual-mode split without first establishing that this specific product actually has two latency-distinct interaction modes.

> **Why does the Document Chat example explicitly warn against over-engineering?** Because "add RAG" is such a strong pattern-matched response to any document-related question that candidates frequently apply it even when the document(s) would fit entirely within a single context window (§15.5) — the correct answer is sometimes "no additional retrieval infrastructure is needed yet," directly the judgment §6.9 and §47.8 modeled.
