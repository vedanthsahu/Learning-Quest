## 63. Worked Interview Examples II: NotebookLM, Perplexity, Enterprise AI Assistant, AI Customer Support

### 63.1 Design NotebookLM (Strictly Source-Grounded Research Assistant)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** The defining constraint is the opposite of ChatGPT's (§62.2) — the corpus is *strictly bounded* to whatever the user explicitly uploads, and answers must never draw on the model's general trained knowledge beyond those sources (§60.3's stricter-than-baseline grounding). A candidate who proposes standard RAG grounding instructions (§23.9) without addressing this stricter requirement has missed the product's core value proposition. **Architecture (Step 6):** Naive-to-advanced RAG (§23.2-23.3) over a small, user-specific corpus (not a large shared knowledge base) — corpus size per user is typically modest, so the retrieval-scale challenges of §21.5's ANN algorithms matter far less here than getting citation-level grounding (§23.9) verifiably strict. **Evaluation (Step 7):** Faithfulness/groundedness (§29.6) is not just one metric among several — it is close to the entire evaluation story for this product, since the value proposition collapses if the system ever answers from outside the provided sources. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Strict grounding directly trades against helpfulness on questions the sources don't fully cover — an over-conservative system that refuses to synthesize or infer anything beyond literal source content becomes frustratingly limited, requiring careful, evaluated calibration (§35.7's "say I don't know" tradeoff) between strictness and usefulness.

### 63.2 Design Perplexity (AI Search Engine)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** Unlike NotebookLM's closed corpus, the corpus here is the live, continuously-changing web — retrieval freshness (§58.3) is a hard requirement no static pre-indexed vector database (§22) can satisfy alone. **Architecture (Step 6):** Real-time web retrieval (likely a hybrid of a search index and reranking, §58.3's inference) feeding grounded, cited generation (§23.9) — directly Agentic or Adaptive RAG (§23.5-23.6) territory when a query requires synthesizing multiple sources, versus simpler single-pass RAG for straightforward factual queries. **Latency budget (Step 4):** Users expect search-engine-like responsiveness, tighter than a "deep research" multi-minute mode (§58.4) — a strong answer explicitly distinguishes a fast, single-pass search mode from an optional slower, more thorough research mode, mirroring the real, documented product distinction (§58.4). **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Retrieval breadth (searching more sources) versus latency — every additional source considered adds both retrieval time and reranking cost (§21.7), directly the tradeoff Adaptive RAG's query-complexity routing (§23.5) exists to manage rather than applying maximum retrieval effort uniformly to every query.

### 63.3 Design an Enterprise AI Assistant (Internal Knowledge + Actions)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** Almost certainly multi-tenant (or at minimum multi-department) from day one, with real access-control requirements (§13.4, §54.4) that a consumer product doesn't have to the same degree — a candidate who doesn't ask "is this one company's data, or does it serve multiple customers/departments with different permissions?" has missed the single most architecture-determining question for this product category. **Architecture (Step 6):** RAG over an internal document corpus (§47) plus tool calling for internal systems (§49, e.g., ticketing, HR, CRM lookups) plus RBAC-gated tool access (§53.4, §54.4's structural tenant/role scoping) — directly Nova's Stage 4/6/10/11 architecture (§47, §49, §53-54), making this question essentially "describe Nova's enterprise-stage architecture from first principles." **Security (Step 9):** This is where the interview answer should spend disproportionate time relative to a consumer product — policy engines (§30.7), least-privilege tool scoping (§30.6), and audit logging (§31.3) are core requirements, not afterthoughts. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Broad tool access increases the assistant's usefulness but directly increases the attack surface and blast radius of any single compromised or manipulated interaction (§13.6) — enterprise deployments should explicitly scope tool access tightly per role rather than granting broad access for convenience.

### 63.4 Design AI Customer Support (Automated Ticket Resolution)

**Hidden constraint (Step 2):** A hard requirement almost every real deployment has: knowing when to escalate to a human, not just how to answer well — an answer that only addresses "how does it answer questions" and never addresses "how does it know when NOT to answer" has missed a defining requirement of this product category specifically. **Architecture (Step 6):** RAG over support documentation/FAQ/prior tickets (§47) plus tools for account/order actions (§49, directly Nova's Stage 4/6 pattern) plus an explicit escalation/confidence mechanism — closely related to §35.7's uncertainty-expression mitigation, but productized here as a first-class routing decision (route to human) rather than just a hedged answer. **Evaluation (Step 7):** Beyond faithfulness (§29.6), resolution rate and — critically — *escalation accuracy* (did it correctly identify cases it shouldn't handle) is a metric this product needs that a pure knowledge-assistant doesn't emphasize as heavily. **Key tradeoff (Step 12):** Automation rate (resolving more tickets without human involvement) directly trades against risk of a wrong or unauthorized action reaching a real customer (§30.6's tool-abuse and §13.6's action-consequence concerns) — a support bot that auto-issues refunds without limits (§30.10's structural cap example) is a direct, concrete illustration of this tradeoff done wrong.

### 63.5 The Pattern Across All Four Examples

These four examples span a spectrum from strictly closed-corpus (NotebookLM) to fully open-corpus (Perplexity) to internal-and-access-controlled (Enterprise Assistant) to action-taking-with-escalation (Customer Support) — the corpus boundary and action-consequence questions (Step 2) are what should drive a candidate to a meaningfully different architecture for each, even though the underlying mechanism catalog (RAG, tools, guardrails) is identical across all four.

### 63.6 Engineering Intuition

> **What single question most efficiently distinguishes these four products' architectures?** "Is the corpus open or closed, and does the system take real actions with real consequences?" — NotebookLM (closed, no actions), Perplexity (open, no actions), Enterprise Assistant (closed/internal, some actions), Customer Support (closed, actions with real consequences) — four different quadrants of the same two-dimensional question.

> **What would over-engineering a Customer Support answer look like?** Proposing a fully autonomous agent (§25.6's multi-agent orchestration) empowered to take unlimited actions without any escalation mechanism or structural action limits (§30.10) — exactly the failure mode real support-automation incidents actually look like.
